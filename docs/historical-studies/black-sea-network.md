# The Black Sea network runs in reverse

[Historical studies](README.md) · [Selected discoveries](../historical-showcase.md) · [All readings](../../cases/README.md)

In late October 1918 the Black Sea traffic still recorded supplies being sent to the Caucasus. Within a fortnight, it was redirecting disembarkation, arranging troop collection and discussing the sale of a steamer. The recovered messages document the changing use of an established transport system. Ship movements can also be compared with Admiral Albert Hopman's diary, while the *Corcovado* correspondence can be followed into the contemporary shipping register.

## Supplies for the Caucasus

The [Sevastopol dispatch, p85 A1](../../cases/caucasus/sevastopol_supply_dispatch.md), reports:

> Nachschubstelle Sewastopol sandte 27 / 10 / 18 an Kaukasusdeleg. folg. Mengen ab: 16400 kg Zucker, 16300 kg Hartfutter, 24200 kg Kartoffeln, 4900 kg Hirse, 8700 kg frisches Gemuese.

| Commodity | Quantity reported dispatched |
|---|---:|
| Sugar | 16,400 kg |
| Hard feed | 16,300 kg |
| Potatoes | 24,200 kg |
| Millet | 4,900 kg |
| Fresh vegetables | 8,700 kg |

All 148 positions decipher without a source change or an erased-coordinate fill, including the five quantities. The body dates the dispatch to 27 October; the heading is dated 30 October and the interception 1 November. This is the first of two advertised parts, so the table represents the surviving part of the consignment list. The message reports dispatch from Sevastopol.

An [October shipping message](../../cases/caucasus/october_caucasus.md) describes the *Violetta* travelling from Braila toward Poti with weapons, ammunition, food, petrol and courier luggage. Some quantities remain damaged. Together, the dispatches show a route serving military equipment, transport fuel and subsistence. Our editions restore printed groups lost in machine extraction and keep the commodity readings attached to their surviving quantities.

## Changed ports and reception arrangements

An instruction originating on 1 November changes the disembarkation point for the text's “Siegfried troops” from **Constanza to Odessa**. The continuation directs ships from Constantinople to Odessa or Nikolaiev according to the cited distribution instructions. The [three p98 records](../../cases/military/black_sea_disembarkation.md) preserve two receptions of that continuation. Recovered source rows make both readable, and the comparison supports four local letter repairs. The differing transmitted spellings of the second port remain in the exact texts.

The [five-part routing order on pp88–89](../../cases/military/black_sea_evacuation_routes.md) also addresses reception after disembarkation. It forbids further traffic to Braila, calls for onward movement in whole trains, names Berlin for civilians and requests early, accurate notice to arrange accommodation and transport. The conditional p89 A3 continuation adds a military-office destination followed by an Ersatzbataillon and 2nd Guards Regiment designation. Three characters and the proposed shortened spelling remain unresolved. The [1918 Berlin directory](../../experiments/p89_destination_continuation/HISTORY.md) independently places the 2. Garderegiment zu Fuß in Berlin.

These instructions distinguish between troops and civilians and coordinate several successive stages of travel. Port selection alone was insufficient: the receiving stations also needed numbers and notice, while inland offices had to arrange accommodation and onward movement.

The armistice of Mudros, signed on 30 October, changed the conditions under which this system operated. It opened the Straits and Black Sea access to the Allies, granted access to ports and control over wireless and cable stations, and required Germans and Austrians to leave Ottoman territory. [Mudros, clauses I, VIII, XII and XIX](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/weimar-germany-1918-1933/ghdi%3Adocument-4012).

## Asgard comes back for the troops

The [p155 message to the Kress route](../../cases/context/p155.md), originating on 7 November and received early on 8 November, expects *Asgard* at Poti within a few days to collect troops. The reconstructed second part reports:

> Asgard Asgard, Tresivolus Tresivolus, 7.11. hier eingelaufen.

The telegram repeats both ship names and leaves the arrival port as *hier*, “here.” Hopman's diary identifies that port as Sevastopol. Its entries record the arrival of *Asgard* and *Tresivolos* on 7 November, the decision to unload them and send them back to Poti, and *Asgard*'s departure for Poti on 9 November. The diary's spelling *Tresivolos* differs from the telegram's `TRESIVOLUS`. [Diary comparison, printed pp637 and 639](../../data/reviews/hopman_context.json); [Baumgart's edition at the Bavarian State Library](https://digitale-sammlungen.de/en/details/bsb00042051).

The two sources establish different stages of the movement: the telegram anticipates collection at Poti, while the diary records arrival at Sevastopol and the subsequent return voyage. Four possible reconstructions of the damaged telegram frame remain in the case edition; the historical identification of the movement does not choose among them.

## A ship becomes an asset to be sold

On 9 November a message to the Mediterranean Division at Nicolajeff proposes terms for selling *Corcovado* to Turkey on behalf of the Hamburg-Amerika Line. The vessel's price is **10 million**, with no currency specified. Inventory and materials are valued separately at cost less wear; personal possessions and coal are excluded. The terms address seizure by Germany's enemies, interest and recovery of the ship for nonpayment. [The six-record Corcovado edition](../../cases/naval/corcovado_sale_terms.md).

The next day's message reports a Turkish crew and captain and failed radio contact with Constantinople. A [15 November instruction](../../cases/naval/black_sea_shipping.md) makes transfer dependent on the payment condition. These records show the German side attempting to preserve an asset's value while specifying who would bear the risks of seizure and default. They establish the proposed conditions and the reported crewing arrangement; completion of the ten-million transaction remains unconfirmed.

The **1919–20 Lloyd's Register of Shipping, Volume II—Steamers, with supplements**, provides a later record of the same vessel. [Main entry 27246](https://archive.org/details/HECROS1920ST/page/n1016/mode/1up) lists *Sueh*, formerly *Corcovado*, under a Turkish flag. Its correction refers explicitly to [supplementary entry 34203](https://archive.org/details/HECROS1920ST/page/n1294/mode/1up), which restores the name *Corcovado* and records **French Government** ownership and a **French** flag. The cross-reference establishes the vessel's identity through these changes, although it supplies neither the transfer date nor an account of the intercepted sale proposal. [Register review](../../data/reviews/lloyds_corcovado_context.json).

## The guard at Tiflis

A [Foreign Office message of 25 November to General von Kress](../../cases/caucasus/p218_A.md) addresses the position of the German guard at Tiflis:

> Die Alliierten haben bisher die Zurueckziehung der deutschen Schutzwache aus Tiflis nicht verlangt. Auswaertiges Amt.

The preceding parts paraphrase Article XII of the 11 November armistice. The [treaty text](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1919Parisv02/d1) made withdrawal from former Russian territories dependent on the moment judged appropriate by the Allies in light of local conditions. This provision identifies the telegram's damaged article reference. The statement that withdrawal from Tiflis had not yet been demanded is the Foreign Office's report of how the provision then applied.

Shipping orders, asset disposal and the Tiflis correspondence disclose different administrative arrangements within the wider withdrawal. The sources distinguish proposed sailings from recorded departures, and evacuation from continued presence under an armistice condition. Our connected edition makes those distinctions available for comparison across messages previously encountered as separate cipher problems.
