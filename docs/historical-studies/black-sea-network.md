# The Black Sea network runs in reverse

[Historical studies](README.md) · [Selected discoveries](../historical-showcase.md) · [All readings](../../cases/README.md)

**The clearest cargo in this collection is a consignment of food.** A Sevastopol supply office reports dispatching sugar, fodder, potatoes, millet and fresh vegetables to the Caucasus delegation on 27 October 1918. Every quantity survives intact. Within the following fortnight, other messages are changing disembarkation ports, calling ships back for troops, and negotiating the disposal of a steamer. Read together, they show a maritime system being redirected from sustaining an overseas presence to managing its withdrawal.

## Five kinds of cargo

The [Sevastopol dispatch, p85 A1](../../cases/caucasus/sevastopol_supply_dispatch.md), reads:

> Nachschubstelle Sewastopol sandte 27 / 10 / 18 an Kaukasusdeleg. folg. Mengen ab: 16400 kg Zucker, 16300 kg Hartfutter, 24200 kg Kartoffeln, 4900 kg Hirse, 8700 kg frisches Gemuese.

| Commodity | Quantity reported dispatched |
|---|---:|
| Sugar | 16,400 kg |
| Hard feed | 16,300 kg |
| Potatoes | 24,200 kg |
| Millet | 4,900 kg |
| Fresh vegetables | 8,700 kg |

This is an unusually solid point of entry into the collection: the complete 148-character raw text deciphers without a source change or an erased-coordinate fill. The key fixes the numerals as well as the words. The body date, 27 October, is distinct from the 30 October heading and the 1 November interception. It is the first of two advertised parts, so these five items are the surviving list, not a claim about the entire cargo. The verb reports dispatch; it does not certify arrival.

The list matters because it supplies the everyday substance behind “Caucasus operations.” Men needed vegetables and sugar; the transport system also needed fodder. A nearby [October shipping message](../../cases/caucasus/october_caucasus.md) describes the *Violetta* travelling from Braila toward Poti with a mixed cargo of weapons, ammunition, food, petrol and courier luggage. Some of its quantities remain damaged. Together the messages show that the same maritime connections carried supplies for fighting, transport and ordinary subsistence.

Our contribution here is a complete, checked source edition: printed groups missing from earlier machine extraction have been restored where the source supports them, quantities remain attached to their exact pairs, and the dispatches can be compared without turning uncertain labels into plausible-looking totals.

## A destination can change before a ship arrives

An instruction originating on 1 November changes where the text's “Siegfried troops” are to disembark: **Odessa replaces Constanza**. Its continuation directs the ships from Constantinople to Odessa or Nikolaiev according to the cited distribution instructions. [The three p98 records](../../cases/military/black_sea_disembarkation.md) preserve two versions of that continuation. Restoring source rows omitted by OCR made both readable, and their independent readings support four local letter repairs.

The second copy does more than repeat a destination. It shows the maintenance of an instruction across a communications chain: a changed route had to survive transcription and retransmission before it could affect movement. The two versions retain different spellings of the second port. Keeping that small difference visible prevents a modern place-name correction from masquerading as evidence for a single perfect original.

The armistice of Mudros, signed on 30 October, provides a larger setting for these movements. It opened the Straits and Black Sea access to the Allies, gave them use of ports and control over wireless and cable stations, and required the evacuation of Germans and Austrians from Ottoman territory. The shipping messages should be read against those changing conditions, while their particular routing decisions remain the evidence of the telegrams themselves. [Mudros, clauses I, VIII, XII and XIX](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/weimar-germany-1918-1933/ghdi%3Adocument-4012).

## Asgard comes back for the troops

The [p155 message to the Kress route](../../cases/context/p155.md), originating on 7 November and received early on 8 November, expects *Asgard* at Poti in the next few days to collect troops. Its second part reports:

> Asgard Asgard, Tresivolus Tresivolus, 7.11. hier eingelaufen.

The repeated ship names belong to the telegram. The word **“hier”**, here, is equally important: the message does not name the arrival port in that sentence. Its second part is a reconstructed reading with four retained source-frame alternatives; the repeated *Asgard* also includes a supported completion of one damaged letter.

An independent contemporary witness supplies the geographical context. Admiral Albert Hopman's diary records *Asgard* and *Tresivolos* arriving at Sevastopol on 7 November, a decision to unload and send them back to Poti, and *Asgard*'s departure for Poti on 9 November. The diary's spelling *Tresivolos* differs from the cipher's repeated `TRESIVOLUS`. Our [diary comparison](../../data/reviews/hopman_context.json) records the inspected entries in Winfried Baumgart's edited volume, printed pages 637 and 639, with links to the [Bavarian State Library copy](https://digitale-sammlungen.de/en/details/bsb00042051).

This is corroboration with a precise division of labour. The telegram gives a troop-pickup intention and an arrival report. The diary identifies the port and records the later departure. The diary does not supply missing cipher symbols or prove which of the four source frames is original. Read side by side, however, the records bring the evacuation traffic into much sharper focus.

## A ship becomes an asset to be sold

The [Corcovado correspondence](../../cases/naval/corcovado_sale_terms.md) introduces another way of ending a naval presence. On 9 November a message to the Mediterranean Division at Nicolajeff proposes conditions for selling the steamer to Turkey on behalf of the Hamburg-Amerika Line. It sets the ship's price at **10 million**, leaving the currency unstated. Inventory and materials are to be valued separately, at cost less wear; the captain's and crew's personal property and coal are excluded.

The sale proposal is attentive to a world in which ownership may offer little security. It addresses seizure by Germany's enemies, payment, interest and the right to reclaim the vessel for nonpayment. The next day's message reports a Turkish crew and captain and failed radio contact with Constantinople. A [15 November instruction](../../cases/naval/black_sea_shipping.md) then makes the proposed transfer depend on the payment condition.

These are not merely commercial footnotes to troop movement. They show the attempt to preserve value and allocate risk as German control receded. The texts record proposals, reports and instructions; they do not by themselves establish a completed sale. The exact price is secure, but adding “marks,” “crowns” or another currency would change the document.

## Why the guard at Tiflis was a different question

By 25 November, the Foreign Office was explaining to General von Kress why a German guard could still be in Tiflis. The [three-part p218 A message](../../cases/caucasus/p218_A.md) paraphrases the armistice's treatment of territory formerly belonging to Russia and adds:

> Die Alliierten haben bisher die Zurueckziehung der deutschen Schutzwache aus Tiflis nicht verlangt. Auswaertiges Amt.

Article XII of the 11 November armistice required withdrawal from former Russian territories when the Allies considered the moment appropriate in light of local conditions. That conditional timing is visible in the [independent treaty text](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1919Parisv02/d1), and identifies the damaged article reference in the telegram. The specific statement that withdrawal of the Tiflis guard had not yet been demanded belongs to the message, not to the treaty.

The maritime story therefore ends with several different tempos. Food had been dispatched; ships were redirected; troops awaited collection; a vessel was being negotiated over; a guard remained subject to a particular legal condition. “Withdrawal” was a series of decisions about people, routes, cargo and authority. The recovered traffic allows those decisions to be read together without collapsing them into a single departure date.
