# Supply, evacuation and ship ownership in the Black Sea, October–November 1918

[Historical studies](README.md) · [Selected discoveries](../../README.md#selected-discoveries-and-methods) · [Complete source editions](../../pages/README.md)

**Research, source recovery and historical synthesis: icekylinx and GPT-6 Astra Pro.**

The Black Sea telegrams reveal how a transport system supplying the Caucasus was adapted to withdrawal. Officers had to choose ports with reception and railway capacity, distinguish civilian from military destinations, retain ships for evacuation and allocate the financial risks of changing control. Supply accounts and cargo enquiries continued alongside these arrangements.

A shared reference number connects the five-part evacuation order to its amendment, documenting the reassignment from Constanza to Odessa. The *Asgard* exchange corresponds to an independently recorded voyage between Sevastopol and Poti. The proposed *Corcovado* sale expressly reserves the vessel for evacuation, while successive messages distinguish agreement, Turkish crewing, payment and ownership. A later shipping register records further changes of name and flag.

## Sources and chronology

J. Rives Childs's intercepted collection records the instructions and reports discussed here. Admiral Albert Hopman's contemporary diary, published in Winfried Baumgart's 1971 edition, independently records ship movements. Diplomatic transmissions of the armistices and *Lloyd's Register* supply the political and commercial context.

## Supply during withdrawal

The clearest quantitative evidence is [p85 A1](../../pages/085.md#source-p85-a1), reporting that Sevastopol's supply office sent provisions to the Caucasus delegation on 27 October:

| Commodity | Quantity reported dispatched |
| --- | ---: |
| Sugar | 16,400 kg |
| Hard feed | 16,300 kg |
| Potatoes | 24,200 kg |
| Millet | 4,900 kg |
| Fresh vegetables | 8,700 kg |

All 148 plaintext positions, including the quantities, decode without substitutions or erased-coordinate fills. The figures belong to the surviving first part of two advertised parts: a dispatch on 27 October, reported on 30 October and intercepted on 1 November.

An earlier [*Violetta* cargo message, p69 B](../../pages/069.md#source-p69-b1), describes transport from Braila for Poti of weapons, ammunition, food, petrol and courier luggage. Its damaged quantities remain conditional. This mixed cargo shows the range of work performed by the route before the withdrawal orders: military equipment, fuel and subsistence travelled through the same transport system.

Commercial administration continued during withdrawal. The [3 November enquiry about tobacco at Poti](../../pages/113.md#source-p113-c) reports readiness for loading and asks when loading is possible. Its literal quantity, `2400`, remains dependent on the published numeral assignment. A [wheat enquiry intercepted on 15 November, p198 A](../../pages/198.md#source-p198-a), asks for the purchase price, freight and transport insurance for delivery at the end of September. Its vessel names and quantity remain unresolved. Cargo planning and the settlement of earlier supply accounts thus persisted during evacuation.

## Port reassignment: FR 52751 to FR 52787

The five-part order begins with [p88 C1](../../pages/088.md#source-p88-c1): “Siegfried” is to go to Constanza, other traffic to Odessa. Its sentence continues in [p89 A2](../../pages/089.md#source-p89-a2), providing Nikolaiev if Odessa can no longer receive the traffic and mentioning railway conditions in the allocation of ships. The order tied the allocation of maritime traffic to reception capacity and inland movement. “Siegfried” remains an unidentified name in this opening.

The order's identity is supplied by its ending. [p90 A5](../../pages/090.md#source-p90-a5) closes with the Feldeisenbahnchef and **RM. 2. Gross B. FR. 52751. 31.10.** The [p98 A1 amendment](../../pages/098.md#source-p98-a1) explicitly cites **FR 52751** and orders that the “Siegfried troops,” too, disembark at Odessa instead of Constanza. Its continuation sends all ships from Constantinople to Odessa or Nikolaiev under the cited distribution instructions and closes with **FR 52787**. The two p98 receptions preserve their distinct spellings of Nikolaiev, `NIKILJEW` and `NIKOLAJEW`.

The references distinguish three stages: **FR 50749**, cited at the beginning of p88 C1, is an earlier instruction; **FR 52751** is the assembled order; **FR 52787** revises its destination. The administrative ending connects the dispersed parts to this sequence.

The remaining parts define the reception arrangements. The reconstructed [p89 A3](../../pages/089.md#source-p89-a3) prohibits further traffic to Braila, turns steamers back and requires onward movement in whole trains. Because the source explicitly lacks twenty groups, this passage depends on substantial declared coordinate fills; the conditional military-office and replacement-battalion destination remains locally uncertain. The firmer [p89 A4](../../pages/089.md#source-p89-a4) names Berlin for civilians, requires separate enquiry about intact troop formations and requests early, accurate notice for accommodation and onward transport. The [distribution list](../../pages/090.md#source-p90-a5) reaches reception stations in Ukraine as well as railway, military and naval offices. The order treats evacuation as a connected sea-and-rail operation, with different categories of travellers requiring different arrangements.

## Armistice terms and the Sevastopol–Poti voyage

The diplomatic transmission of [the Mudros armistice](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1918Supp01v01/d368), received by the United States on 1 November, places these decisions in a changing institutional setting. Signed on 30 October and effective at noon on 31 October, it opened the Straits and Black Sea access, provided Allied use of Ottoman ports, and placed wireless and cable stations under Allied control, excepting Turkish Government messages. Clause XIX required Germans and Austrians, including civilians, to leave Ottoman territory within a month, with an allowance for remote districts. These terms gave urgency to the transport and communication arrangements recorded in the telegrams.

The [7 November message for the Kress route, p155 A](../../pages/155.md#source-p155-a1), expects *Asgard* at Poti within days to collect troops. Its second part reports that *Asgard* and *Tresivolus* arrived “here” on 7 November. The port is absent from the ciphertext. In his diary entry for that day, Hopman records their arrival at **Sevastopol at 09:00**, followed by a decision to unload them and send them back to Poti. His 9 November entry records *Asgard* leaving for Poti. See [printed p637](https://digitale-sammlungen.de/en/view/bsb00042051?page=647) and [p639](https://digitale-sammlungen.de/en/view/bsb00042051?page=649) of Baumgart's edition.

Hopman's entries distinguish arrival, the turnaround decision and departure; collection at Poti remains prospective. The diary's *Tresivolos* differs from the telegram's repeated `TRESIVOLUS`. Four damaged-frame reconstructions remain compatible with the voyage chronology, as documented in the [diary review](../../data/reviews/hopman_context.json).

## Corcovado: evacuation terms inside a proposed sale

The [9 November instructions to the Mediterranean Division at Nicolajeff](../../pages/165.md#source-p165-a1) seek terms for selling *Corcovado* to Turkey on behalf of the Hamburg-Amerika Line. The hull price is **10 million**, directly present in the cipher reading; no currency is stated. Inventory and materials are valued separately at cost less wear, while personal property and coal are excluded. Turkey is to bear seizure risk from Germany's enemies from the conclusion of the sale. Interest is to begin no later than seven months afterward, and a right to recover the vessel for nonpayment is reserved.

[The final surviving part, p165 A5](../../pages/165.md#source-p165-a5), reserves the ship exclusively or primarily for transporting Germans away. It also reserves a German right of first refusal on resale, prohibits sale to a non-German shipping company, and directs that the telegram be forwarded to Constantinople only in cipher. These proposed clauses link the sale to continued evacuation capacity and future commercial claims while reallocating immediate risks.

The [10 November report](../../pages/173.md#source-p173-a1) states that the Mediterranean Division or its representative has left Nikolaiev, *Corcovado* is operating with a Turkish crew and captain, and radio contact with Constantinople is unavailable. Its cited reference ends `?24`, while the 9 November instruction ends `8?3`: the surviving messages concern the same negotiation but have no matching request–reply reference. Turkish crewing describes operational control; payment and title remained separate questions. The cause of the failed radio contact is unreported.

The [15 November message](../../pages/200.md#source-p200-b1) separates an internal order from its diplomatic explanation. The Marineamt orders the sale stopped if unsettled, or ownership transfer withheld if an agreement already exists. For presentation to Turkey, the continuation invokes the shipowner's requirement for cash payment **in Germany before ownership transfer**. Berlin's negotiating position shifts from the earlier provisions for interest and recovery on nonpayment toward securing payment before relinquishing title.

The [11 November German armistice](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1919Parisv02/d1) sharpened the surrounding constraints. Article XXVI preserved the blockade and liability of German merchant ships at sea to capture; XXIX required German evacuation of Black Sea ports; XXXIII prohibited transfers of German merchant shipping to neutral flags after signature. These provisions form the legal setting; their application to this transaction, including Turkey's treatment under the neutral-flag clause, remains unresolved.

## Corcovado's later flags and ownership

The [1919–20 *Lloyd's Register*, main entry 27246](https://archive.org/details/HECROS1920ST/page/n1016/mode/1up), records **Sueh, formerly Corcovado**, under a Turkish flag; its owner field is blank. An explicit correction points to [supplementary entry 34203](https://archive.org/details/HECROS1920ST/page/n1294/mode/1up), which restores **Corcovado**, with **French Government** ownership and a **French** flag. The explicit cross-reference identifies the vessel through these changes.

The volume covers 1 July 1919 to 30 June 1920; it leaves the dates of these changes and payment of the proposed ten million unresolved. The [visual review](../../data/reviews/lloyds_corcovado_context.json) gives the title page, entry locators and independent checks. A separately verified [French National Archives finding-aid entry](https://www.siv.archives-nationales.culture.gouv.fr/siv/UD/FRAN_IR_023544/d_1_2_14_10)—**20060046/15, dossier 210**, “Souhl – ex Corcovado”—gives **31 January 1919** without identifying the event. Its underlying case documents remain uninspected.

## The Tiflis guard and conditional withdrawal

The [Foreign Office message to Kress of 25 November, p218 A](../../pages/218.md#source-p218-a3), reports that the Allies have not yet demanded withdrawal of the German guard at Tiflis. Its paraphrase corresponds to [Article XII of the German armistice](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1919Parisv02/d1): the timing of return from former Russian territory depended on Allied judgment of local conditions. The Foreign Office was reporting how that condition applied to the guard on 25 November.

Port allocation, troop collection, commercial safeguards and guard duty followed different administrative timetables. The evacuation orders reassigned destinations, the sale proposals reserved capacity and commercial claims, and the Tiflis message reported no Allied demand for the guard's withdrawal.
